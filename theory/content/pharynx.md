## General overview {#overview covers="pharynx.o1"}

The Pharynx is an ~12cm muscular tube found posteriorly to the nasal cavity and oral cavity that acts as a common 'highway' for food, water, and air to be transported. 

The structure itself can be difficult to comprehend as there are lots of critical muscles, fascial features, and nerves that are in and around this area. There are significant clinical implications linked to its functions as well so keep those in mind. 

Sources: own

## Attachments and location of the pharynx {#attach-and-locate covers="pharynx.s1 pharynx.s2 pharynx.o6 pharynx.s11"}

The pharynx sits beneath the base of the skull and behind the nasal cavity, oral cavity, and larynx. It sits anteriorly to the prevertebral muscles (and associated fascia). Laterally to the pharynx sit critical neurovascular bundles containing the carotids (common, internal, external), internal jugular vein, vagus nerve, and sympathetic trunk in close proximity. 

Sources: cunn ch. 17 p. 217

### Superior and inferior attachments of the pharynx 
<!-- Skull attachment, peripheral attachments, inferior attachment-->

The pharynx attaches at the base of the skull superiorly via numerous attachment points. These include:
- the pharyngeal tubercle occipital bone
- quadrate part of the petrous temporal bone
- medial pterygoid plates, hamulus (of the sphenoid) and pterygomandibular raphe
- Cartilaginous portion of the auditory tube 
- Anterior margin of carotid canal

A good portion of the superior attachment isn't actually muscular, rather that upper border of the superior constrictor leaves a gap that is filled in by the pharyngobasilar fascia, auditory tube, tensor and levator palati muscles, and various other neurovascular components. 

Inferiorly, the pharynx ends at about C6, and is continous with the oesophagus, a muscular tube connecting to the stomach at the level of T10 just below the diaphragm. 

Sources: last ch 6

## Superstructure of the pharynx {#superstructure covers="pharynx.o1"}

The pharynx is split into 3 sections: the Nasopharynx, Oropharynx, and Hypopharynx (or Laryngopharynx). These pharyngeal sections are delineated by several anatomic structures. 

Sources: own; cunn ch. 17, pp. 216-217

### The nasopharynx {#pharyngeal-recess covers="pharynx.s8"}

The nasopharynx is the section of the pharynx located behind the nasal cavity. Its borders are as follows: 

:::keypoint Borders of nasopharynx (High Yield)
- Anterior: Choanae posterior of the nasal cavity 
- Posterior and superior: a continuous curved surface from base of skull to posterior pharyngeal wall 
- Inferior: the pharyngeal isthmus denoted by the tip of the soft palate
:::

The contents of the nasopharynx are linked to its functions of moving air from respiration and regulating pressure with the middle ear. 

:::keypoint Contents of nasopharynx (High Yield)
- pharyngeal tonsil: bulging out in the superior mucous membrane of the roof
- opening of auditory tube: a connection to the middle ear
- Torus Tubarius (tubal ridge): the ridge overlying the auditory tube opening
- Tubal tonsil: another component of Waldeyer's ring
- Salpingopharyngeal fold: the fold covering the salpingopharyngeus muscle 
- Pharyngeal recess: Also known as the fossa of Rosenmuller, the most common site for nasopharyngeal carcinoma origin
:::

Sources: cunn ch.17, pp. 219-222; own

### The oropharynx {#oropharynx covers="pharynx.s10 pharynx.c5"}

The oropharynx is the section of the pharynx located behind the oral cavity. It forms the bridge between the oral cavity and the rest of the aerodigestive tract. The borders of the oropharynx are as follows 

:::keypoint Borders of oropharynx (High Yield)
- Anterior: Oropharyngeal isthmus (palatoglossal folds)
- Superior: Pharyngeal isthmus (tip of the soft palate)
- Inferior: Tip of the epiglottis 
:::

The contents of the oropharynx are as follows

:::keypoint Features of the oropharynx (High Yield)
- Lingual tonsil 
- Palatoglossal arches 
- Palatopharyngeal arches 
- Palatine Tonsil 
- Epiglottic valleculae 
- Median/Lateral Glosso-epiglottic Folds 
:::

### The laryngopharynx {#laryngopharynx covers="pharynx.s9 pharynx.s11 pharynx.c3 pharynx.c1"}

The laryngopharynx is the most inferior pharyngeal portion, lying posterior to the larynx, emptying out like a funnel into the oesophagus. 

:::keypoint Borders of the laryngpharynx (High Yield)
- Superior: Tip of epiglottis  
- Inferior: Inferior border of cricoid cartilage 
- Posterior and Lateral: middle and inferior constrictor muscles 
- Anterior: laryngeal inlet, piriform recesses. 
:::

:::keypoint Features of the laryngopharynx 
- Piriform (or Pyriform) recesses 
- Killian's dehiscence 
:::

:::clinical Killian's dehiscence 
Killian's dehiscence is a triangle shaped area in the inferior pharyngeal constrictor where the oblique fibres of the thyropharyngeal part and the horizontal fibres of the cricopharyngeal part. 

The area between these muscle fibre orientations is very thin, which makes it the primary site for the formation of Zenker's diverticulum, an acquired pharyngeal pouch. 

Key symptoms of this are dysphagia and unpleasant swallowing, trapped food, and halitosis. 

Sources: own
:::

## Pharynx musculature and tissue layers {#musculature covers="pharynx.o3 pharynx.s7"}

The pharyngeal wall is formed from 4 major layers. From superficial to deep it is: mucosal, submucosal, muscular, fascial. 

The mucosa of the nasopharynx consists of respiratory epithelium (ciliated stratified squamous epithelium), then non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium for the oropharynx and laryngopharynx portions. 

The submucosal layer contains the pharyngobasilar fascia which lines the internal surface of the pharyngeal muscles. The pharyngobasilar fascia attaches the pharynx to its surrounding structures like the base of the skull, auditory tubes, and choanae. 

:::note 
Some sources suggest 5 layers (mucosa, submucosa, pharyngobasilar fascia, musculature, buccopharyngeal fasia). However, for simplicity sake the submucosa and pharyngobasilar layer can be combined together. 
:::

Sources: own; cunn ch. 17, pp. 216-217

## Fascia and membranes {#fascia covers="pharynx.s3"}

### Fasciae of the pharynx 

A number of important fasciae are attached to and associated with the pharynx. These include the pharyngobasilar, buccopharyngeal (pretracheal), and alar to name a few. 

The pharyngobasilar fascia is a "suspensory" fascia that sits atop the nasopharyngeal portion of the pharynx suspending it from the base of the skull. It serves two key purposes: to suspend the pharynx from the base of the skull, and to keep the nasopharynx open to allow airflow. Otherwise, the nasopharynx could be at risk of collapse and the individual could suffocate. 

There is a gap between the superior pharyngeal constrictor and the base of the skull filled by this fascia, which is called the sinus of Morgagni. This gap is clinically significant as it is a natural weak point, which allows the migration of nasopharygneal carcinomas and for their invasion into surrounding skull base features. 

The buccopharyngeal fascia is commonly confused with the pharyngobasilar fascia but it exists in a separate tissue plane. 

:::clinical Sinus of Morgagni
The sinus of morgagni refers to the area around the top of the pharynx where numerous structures pass including the auditory tube, tensor/levator palati, and the ascending palatine artery. 

As a natural fascial deficit, tumours like nasopharyngeal carcinomas can spread laterally through and invade the auditory tube to middle ear. 

Produces trotter's syndrome which is characterised by a trifecta of: 
- conductive hearing loss due to auditory tube blockage 
- Ipsilateral pain in mandibular nerve (trigeminal neuralgia) 
- Asymmetry or paralysis of soft palate
Sources: own
:::

Sources: own; cunn ch. 17, pp. 216-217
